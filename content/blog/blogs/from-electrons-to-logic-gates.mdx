## From electricity to switching

Pikuma's course starts closer to the physical world than most computer architecture material. It covers atoms, electrons, conductors and insulators, current, voltage, resistance, electric fields, and the difference between electron flow and conventional current. From there, it introduces components such as resistors, capacitors, diodes, relays, and transistors.

One useful idea is that a digital signal is still an electrical signal. A computer interprets ranges of voltage as logical values, but the underlying system is physical. A transistor can act as a controlled switch: one signal determines whether another path is open or closed. With enough switches working together, simple electrical behavior becomes logic.

## Why logic gates matter

Logic gates are where the connection between electronics and computation becomes clear. An **AND** gate only produces a high output when both inputs are high. An **OR** gate responds when either input is high, and so one. If you calcualte all of the possible combinations it turns out to be 16 different types, where some are actually considered useless while others are actually a lot more useful than one might think at first glance. Take the buffer gate for example. The buffer takes in a signal- a 1 or a 0 and just repeats it. This may seem useless but it's actually very useful in amplifying a poor signal and making sure it is not lost. These small rules can be represented with truth tables, but they can also be built from relays or transistors.

The most important gate in this progression is **NAND**, a gate that the book emphasized the importance of and what the entire course is based on. NAND is functionally complete, meaning that NOT, AND, and OR can all be constructed from NAND gates alone. That makes it possible to describe an entire digital system using one primitive operation. Combining gates leads to multiplexers, adders, latches, registers, memory, and an arithmetic logic unit.

Petzold's *Code* made this progression especially intuitive. Its examples connect batteries, switches, telegraphs, relays, codes, and Boolean logic before building toward clocks, gates, memory, CPU control signals, registers, buses, and an ALU. The physical details make the abstractions feel less magical: a processor is ultimately coordinating many controlled paths for electrical signals.

## Building the computer

The Nand2Tetris course takes the logic-gate idea and turns it into a construction project. Starting with NAND, the projects build Boolean chips, arithmetic circuits, memory, a CPU, and the Hack computer. The second half continues upward into machine language, an assembler, a virtual machine, a compiler, and an operating system.

Nand2Tetris intentionally begins at the digital abstraction level rather than teaching semiconductor physics. That made it a useful complement to Pikuma and *Code*: Pikuma helped explain the physical components and signals, *Code* connected those ideas through the history of computing, and Nand2Tetris provided a systematic way to implement the abstractions. All three of these materials provided a solid foundation for me to implement in my future projects.

The biggest lesson for me is that software begins with physical behavior that can be controlled, represented, combined, and remembered. Electrons and fields become signals. The signals go through switches, which then turn into  countless configurations of logic gates. These logic gates are the structure that executes a program and the code that I have been writing to solve problems.
